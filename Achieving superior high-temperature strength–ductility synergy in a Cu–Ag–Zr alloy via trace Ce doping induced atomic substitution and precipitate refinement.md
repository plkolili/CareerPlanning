   2  Abstract: High-conductivity copper alloys often suffer from severe degradation in strength and ductility at elevated temperatures, limiting their applications under extreme thermal conditions. Here, we demonstrate that the addition of a trace amount of Ce (0.2 wt.%) to a Cu–Ag–Zr alloy leads to exceptional high-temperature mechanical properties without significantly compromising thermal conductivity. Ce efficiently scavenges free oxygen and metallic impurities, leading to grain boundary purification and dispersion strengthening through nano-sized rare earth oxide particles. Consequently, this significantly improves the alloy's high-temperature strength and ductility while preserving its high thermal conductivity. Concurrently, the addition of Ce refines submicron secondary phase particles and increases their number density, resulting in a substantial decrease in the final grain size due to the recrystallization of PSN nuclei. Furthermore, Ce suppresses both discontinuous precipitation and coarsening of nanoscale Ag precipitates, thereby reinforcing the Zener pinning effect of the secondary phase on grain boundaries and inhibiting grain coarsening during recrystallization. These mechanisms work together to refine the grains, prevent grain coarsening during heating, and enhance the high-temperature mechanical properties of the alloy. This work establishes a viable strategy for optimizing the high-temperature performance of Cu–Ag–Zr alloys without compromising thermal conductivity, offering direct guidance for their application in high temperature service environments. Key words: copper alloy; rare earth; second phase particle; thermal conductivity; high-temperature strength and plasticity; thermal stability; in-situ high temperature SEM/EBSD

Introduction Advanced thermal management systems, such as the combustion chambers in high-thrust liquid rocket engines, present a formidable materials challenge: they demand structural materials capable of withstanding extreme temperatures while efficiently dissipating immense heat fluxes[1-3]. Key components like the inner wall of high-thrust liquid rocket engine combustion chambers require copper alloys that not only possess outstanding thermal conductivity to manage extreme heat fluxes from 3000–4000 °C gases but also retain excellent mechanical strength and ductility at elevated temperatures[4]. This combination of properties is critical for ensuring structural integrity and operational reliability under extreme thermal–mechanical cycling. Alloys, such as NARloy-Z (Cu–3Ag–0.5Zr), have been established as benchmark materials for their reliable performance [5-8]. However, the emergence of next-generation alloys such as GRCop-84 highlights the continuous quest for enhanced high-temperature capabilities [9-13]. A central challenge in this endeavor lies in overcoming the inherent trade-off between high thermal conductivity, which necessitates a pure copper matrix, and high-temperature strength, which typically requires the introduction of thermally stable second phase particles [14-18]. To strengthen copper alloys effectively, a widely adopted approach involves incorporating hard and insoluble second phase particles [14, 16, 18-23]. Although the addition of a second phase (e.g., intermetallic compounds, ceramic particles) to reinforce the copper matrix also results in a reduced thermal conductivity, this reduction is significantly less pronounced compared to when alloying elements are dissolved in the solid solution state. The stability of the microstructure and mechanical properties in Cu–Ag–Zr alloys at high temperatures primarily relies on the morphology, size and volume fraction of the Cu₄AgZr particle phases at micron/sub-micron levels and nanoscale Ag                    4  precipitates [2, 7, 8]. Micron/submicron Cu₄AgZr particles primarily serve to pin dislocations, restrict grain boundary movement under high-temperature loading, and impede grain growth [12]. The spherical shape, more dispersed and denser distribution, and smaller size of Cu₄AgZr particles generally enhance the high-temperature strength and ductility of the alloy [24, 25]. Ag precipitation occurs in two ways: one involves spherical particles dispersed within the grains at around 10 nanometers, known as continuous/coherent precipitation; the other consists of non-spherical particles ranging from tens to hundreds of nanometers at grain boundaries, termed as discontinuous/incoherent precipitation [19, 26-29]. The discontinuous/incoherent precipitation of Ag helps in raising the recrystallization temperature and improving high-temperature mechanical properties, while also preserving the excellent electrical and thermal conductivity of the Cu–Ag–Zr alloys due to the coherent phase interface [5, 6, 26]. However, upon prolonged exposure to temperatures exceeding 500 °C, these continuous/coherent Ag precipitates become unstable[8, 30]. Accelerated elemental diffusion, particularly of Ag along grain boundaries, triggers coarsening of precipitates and a transition to discontinuous precipitation [12]. This microstructural degradation leads to a pronounced decline in high-temperature strength, ductility, and thermal conductivity. Therefore, suppressing elemental diffusion and the concomitant coarsening of Ag is paramount for enhancing high-temperature performance of the Cu–Ag–Zr alloys. Metals primarily rely on electrons for thermal conduction. Introducing impurity atoms (e.g., Ca, S, O) into a copper matrix induces lattice distortion, which enhances electron scattering, reduces the electron mean free path, and consequently decreases the alloy's thermal conductivity [9, 10, 31]. Impurity elements in copper alloys are primarily categorized into non-metallic elements (such as oxygen) and metallic elements (such as calcium), originating from raw materials, processing                    5  procedures, and environmental contamination [32-34]. These impurities can significantly impair high-temperature mechanical properties and thermal conductivity by disrupting the integrity of the crystal lattice and degrading the characteristics of grain boundaries [35]. For example, oxygen (O) readily forms Cu2O inclusions with copper, which act as strong electron scattering centers, thereby reducing thermal conductivity and weakening the cohesion of grain boundaries, thus promoting intergranular fracture at elevated temperatures[4, 5]. Calcium (Ca) has a large atomic radius, which induces significant lattice distortion upon solid solution and results in a severe decrease in thermal conductivity. Additionally, the segregation of CaO at grain boundaries results in stress concentration at high-temperature interfaces, ultimately compromising high-temperature strength [13].  Rare earth (RE) element microalloying presents a promising strategy to address this multifaceted challenge. In copper alloys, the effect of RE elements has been attributed to both purification and microalloying [21, 22]. Their high chemical reactivity enables them to scavenge detrimental impurities such as oxygen, sulfur, and calcium from the copper matrix and grain boundaries[36, 37]. This purification effect enhances both thermal conductivity and high-temperature ductility. Simultaneously, RE elements can interact with alloying components to form fine, dispersed second phase particles with high melting points[38]. These particles effectively pin grain boundaries and dislocations, thereby improving resistance to recrystallization and grain growth, which stabilizes strength and ductility at high temperatures. Cerium (Ce) is the most abundant light rare-earth element (comprising ~60% of total rare-earth reserves), featuring low cost and well-established industrial refining processes—thereby circumventing the high costs and supply-chain vulnerabilities associated with scarce heavy rare-earth elements (e.g., Y, Gd) and enabling scalable production of high-temperature Cu alloys. The                    6  grain-refining effect of Ce is negligible at concentrations ≤0.05 wt.%, whereas additions of 0.05–0.2 wt.% Ce significantly refine the grain structure of Cu–Ag alloys, with tensile strength attaining a maximum at 0.2 wt.% Ce [34, 35]. Besides, the addition of small amounts of Ce has been shown to enhance the room-temperature tensile strength of Cu alloys without significantly compromising their electrical conductivity [34, 39]. However, the effects of rare earth element incorporation, particularly Ce, on the high-temperature physical and mechanical properties of copper-based alloys remain poorly understood. Given the demanding thermo-mechanical environment experienced by combustion chamber walls, it is essential to systematically investigate how Ce doping influences both the thermal conductivity and high-temperature mechanical behavior of the Cu–Ag–Zr alloy. By utilizing powder metallurgy to introduce trace amounts of Ce, the high-temperature thermal stability of the Cu–3Ag–0.5Zr alloy is successfully improved while maintaining its exceptional thermal conductivity in this study. The addition of trace Ce efficiently eliminates the residual impurities from the matrix, increases the population of submicron Cu₄AgZr particles and refines them at the same time. Additionally, it changes the precipitation pattern of the Ag phase during aging, causing the development of fine, dispersed, and cohesive precipitates throughout the material. Through a combination of atomic-resolution microscopy and in-situ high-temperature SEM/EBSD analysis, we provide direct evidence of how trace Ce doping tailors the precipitation behavior and grain stability, providing insights for designing high-performance copper alloys for extreme environments. 2. Experiments 2.1 Sample preparation In this study, 0.2 wt.% of the rare earth element Ce was added to the NARloy-Z (Cu–3Ag–                   7  0.5Zr) alloy. The raw materials used were high-purity electrolytic copper (purity greater than 99.9%), high-purity silver (purity greater than 99.9%), a Cu-50% Zr (mass fraction, the same below) master alloy, and a Cu-10% Ce master alloy. Because of the extremely low solid solubility of Zr and Ce in Cu, macrosegregation in the alloy cannot be avoided with conventional casting methods [2, 24]. To produce alloys with a fine grain size and dispersed second-phase particles, the alloy is fabricated using gas atomization and powder metallurgy techniques. Pre-alloyed powders of two compositions were prepared using vacuum melting and argon atomization. The raw materials were formulated according to the compositions of the Cu–3Ag–0.5Zr alloy and the Cu–3Ag–0.5Zr–0.2Ce alloy, respectively. The melting and atomization chambers of the vacuum atomization apparatus were evacuated to 1.0×10-2 Pa and maintained for 5 min. After ensuring that the vacuum level did not drop, argon was then filled to standard atmospheric pressure as a shielding gas. A high-frequency induction device was then activated for heating. Once the alloy in the crucible was completely melted, the melt temperature was raised to 1350 ˚C and held for 30 min in preparation for atomization and powder production. Atomization and pulverization were initiated by adjusting the argon atomization pressure to 3.5 MPa. The collected powder was sieved through a 200-mesh sieve, immediately vacuum-packaged, and stored in a vacuum drying oven. The actual composition of the two sets of pre-alloyed powders was determined using a SPECTRO BLUE SOP inductively coupled plasma (ICP) spectrometer. The results are shown in Table 1. The resulting powders were densified using hot isostatic pressing. The powders were first loaded into a steel cylindrical can with internal dimensions of 120 mm × 150 mm. The tap density of the pre-alloyed powder for each component ranged from 5.3 to 5.6 gcm-³. The sealed can was then placed in a hot isostatic pressing furnace for pressurization and heating. The hot isostatic pressing process was held at a holding pressure of 150                    8  MPa and a holding temperature of 830 °C. The pressing and heating processes were synchronized, and the holding time was 2 h. The hot isostatically pressed alloys were then cold rolled. The room temperature cold-rolled samples measured 120 mm × 40 mm × 12 mm, with a total deformation of 80% and a deformation of 10% per pass. The rolled samples were then aged at 400 °C for 30 min. Aging at 400 °C for 30 min for the rolled Cu-Ag-Zr alloy had been demonstrated in previous studies to notably enhance ductility, recover electrical conductivity, and preserve strength [2]. As shown in Fig. 2a, it can be observed that the thermal conductivity of both alloys initially rises and then falls as the temperature increases, peaking at 300 ℃. The incorporation of trace rare earth Ce leads to a slight decrease in thermal conductivity. At room temperature, the thermal conductivity of the Ce-free alloy is 11 Wm-1k-1 higher than that of the Ce-containing alloy. As the test temperature increases, the difference in thermal conductivity between the two alloys gradually decreases. At 500 ℃, the thermal conductivity of the Ce-free alloy is 5 Wm-1k-1 (1.5%) higher than that of the Ce-containing alloy. The good heat transfer ability of the alloy is obviously preserved after adding a small amount of Ce. At 350 ℃, the Ce-free alloy has a high-temperature strength of 512 MPa, while the Ce-containing alloy shows an increase of 65 MPa in strength without a significant decrease in elongation. At 400 ℃, the Ce-containing alloy still has an advantage of nearly 20 MPa in high-temperature strength compared to the Ce-free alloy. When the testing temperature reaches 500 ℃, the strength of the Ce-free alloy decreases to 239 MPa with an increase in plasticity to 32%. On the other hand, the Ce-containing alloy maintains a high strength of 263 MPa with good high-temperature plasticity of about 28%. As can be seen, adding a small amount of Ce to the Cu–Ag–Zr alloy can maintain high-temperature plasticity and thermal conductivity while enhancing high-temperature strength. At the same temperature, the formation of zirconium oxide and cerium oxide exhibits a lower Gibbs free energy than that of calcium oxide. When the local oxygen partial pressure is sufficient to initiate oxidation, Zr and Ce react with oxygen atoms to nucleate the nano-core preferentially, due to their high oxygen affinity, during alloy melting, atomization, sintering, and subsequent heat treatment. Upon reaching the surface of the Zr-Ce-O nano-core, CaO (lattice constant: 4.81 Å) undergoes epitaxial growth on the Zr-Ce-O (Ce0.5Zr0.5O2 lattice constant: 5.26 Å), facilitated by a small lattice mismatch of approximately 8.5%, which is below the typical threshold for epitaxial growth (<10%) [48, 49]. The key lies in the creation of calcium oxide on existing cerium zirconium oxide particles, acting as a substrate to lower the nucleation free energy barrier by reducing the interfacial mismatch, ultimately leading to the achievement of heterogeneous nucleation. The reduced interfacial energy promotes the layered, continuous deposition of a dense Ca-rich shell. Analogous mechanisms have been observed in Al-Ce/Zr-O and Y-Cr-O systems, suggesting broad applicability across multicomponent oxides, characterized by nucleation of high oxygen-affinity elements followed by encapsulation via low diffusion-barrier elements [50, 51]. The XRD analysis in Fig. S3 indicates that the addition of Ce induces a slight rightward shift in the Cu diffraction peak. In the Ce-free alloy, impurity elements such as Ca within the Cu face-centered cubic lattice induce lattice distortion, resulting in an increased interplanar spacing (d) and a decreased diffraction angle (θ), which causes the Cu diffraction peak to shift leftward relative to that of pure copper. Upon the introduction of trace amounts of Ce, impurity elements are effectively scavenged from the Cu lattice, thereby alleviating lattice distortion. Consequently, the interplanar spacing (d) decreases and the diffraction angle (θ) increases, leading to a slight rightward shift in the Cu diffraction peak compared to the Ce-free alloy. The purification mechanism of Ce in copper alloys is primarily attributed to differences in electronegativity and atomic-scale interfacial behavior. Ce exhibits strong chemical selectivity due to its electronegativity of 1.12, which is notably lower than that of Zr (1.33), Cu (1.90) and O (3.44) but marginally higher than Ca (1.00) [55]. With its higher affinity for oxygen compared to copper, cerium can selectively react with non-metallic impurities. As depicted in Fig 9, this reaction leads to the production of thermally stable rare earth oxides, effectively preventing the formation of detrimental inclusions like Cu₂O. Moreover, the atomic radius of Ce (0.182 nm) and Cu (0.128 nm) exhibit a significant difference, limiting its solubility in the copper matrix. As a result, Ce tends to segregate at high energy regions of grain boundaries, where it adsorbs O and Ca impurities, reduces                    34  electron scattering centers, and forms fine spherical oxides below 100 nm in size. This process eliminates lattice distortions induced by impurities and effectively removes harmful elements from grain boundaries, enabling comprehensive purification. The uniform dispersion of rare earth oxides introduces minimal obstruction to electron transport, thereby preserving high thermal conductivity at elevated temperatures and avoiding the degradation typically caused by conventional impurities. On the other hand, the addition of trace amounts of rare earth Ce to the alloy enhances high-temperature plasticity and strength through grain boundary purification and dispersion strengthening mediated by nano-sized rare earth oxide particles. Cerium reacts with impurities such as oxygen and calcium in the alloy, forming thermally stable rare earth oxides that immobilize these impurities and prevent embrittlement caused by their segregation at grain boundaries. The dispersed nano-sized rare earth oxide particles effectively hinder dislocation motion, serve as pinning sites to suppress grain growth, and retard dynamic recovery and recrystallization processes, thus contributing to enhanced high-temperature strength and ductility[22, 56]. This synergistic combination of grain boundary purification and dispersion strengthening results in significantly improved high-temperature mechanical performance in copper alloys. 4.2 Atomic Ce substitution induces precipitate refinement The size, quantity, uniform distribution, and thermal stability of second phase particles play crucial roles in determining the high-temperature microstructural stability of alloys, which subsequently impacts their mechanical performance retention [57, 58]. Ideally, when the alloy exhibits a finer second phase particle size, increased quantity, well-dispersed distribution, and enhanced thermal stability, it tends to demonstrate superior high-temperature mechanical properties[14, 16, 17, 42, 43]. The high-temperature mechanical properties of the alloy are greatly                    35  improved by the addition of Ce, primarily through its influence on the formation of the Cu4AgZr phase and the precipitation of Ag. As depicted in Fig. 11, the Ce-free alloy shows minimal coarsening of the submicron Cu4AgZr phases, which is vital for pinning grain boundaries and preventing grain growth, thereby improving the alloy's high-temperature strength [8]. This means that enhancing thermal stability in the Cu-Ag-Zr alloy could be achieved by creating smaller, more numerous, and better dispersed Cu4AgZr particle phases. By adding minor concentrations of Ce, the volume fraction of the Cu4Ag(ZrCe) phase in the alloy is enhanced without enlarging the phase, as evidenced by the statistical analysis depicted in Fig. 8 and Table 4. In the Ce-free alloy, only 24% of the Cu4AgZr phase sizes are smaller than 200 nm. Conversely, the Ce-containing alloy exhibits a substantial 68% of the Cu4Ag(ZrCe) phase size below 200 nm with identical plastic deformation and aging processes. During the aging process, a considerable number of Cu4Ag(ZrCe) phases smaller than 200 nm are thought to form in Ce-containing alloys, as shown in Fig. 5. While the precipitation of Cu4AgZr phases of this size is rarely observed during aging in the Ce-free alloy. Moreover, the Ce-free alloy contains a volume fraction of 5.8% of the Cu4AgZr phase. Upon the addition of rare earth element Ce, the volume fraction of this phase in the alloy increases to 7.1%. Adding Ce is advantageous for promoting the nucleation of the Cu4Ag(ZrCe) phase, leading to an increase in the volume fraction of this stable phase in the alloy. Based on the atomic-scale compositional analysis in Fig. 4, the occupancies of Cu and Ag atoms remain unchanged in the Cu₄Ag(ZrCe) phase compared to the Cu₄AgZr phase. However, Ce atoms partially substitute for Zr at the Zr lattice sites. This indicates that Ce and Zr compete for occupancy in the same crystallographic positions during the formation of this type of secondary phase. Introducing a more reactive rare earth element can significantly enhance the                    36  nucleation rate of the second phase while hindering its coarsening [59, 60]. A similar competitive mechanism has been identified in Cu-Ni-Co-Si alloys [61, 62]. Our team initially explored increasing the Zr content in the alloy to enhance thermal stability before considering adding trace rare earth elements. The aim is to increase the volume fraction of the submicron Cu4AgZr phase in order to enhance the high-temperature strength and plasticity of the Cu-3Ag-0.5Zr alloy. Increasing the Zr content to 0.8% results in a significant rise in the volume fraction of the submicron Cu4AgZr phase, as depicted in Fig. S4 and Table S1. However, this causes coarsening and uneven distribution of the submicron phase particles, leading to a slight decrease in strength and plasticity of the Cu-3Ag-0.8Zr alloy at high temperatures. The incorporation of the rare earth element Ce not only improves the volume fraction of submicron thermal stable phase particles in the alloy but also ensures their uniform distribution and prevents their coarsening. This synergistically enhances the alloy's high-temperature strength and plasticity. Furthermore, comparing Fig. 5 and Fig. S4 shows that incorporating trace Ce is more beneficial in spheroidizing the submicron second phase particles. This spheroidization aids in minimizing stress concentration during alloy loading, leading to increased strength and plasticity at elevated temperatures. Besides, enhancing the high-temperature mechanical performance of the alloy can be achieved by optimizing the size and volume fraction of Ag precipitates in the alloy, as well as ensuring its stability during the heating process. The addition of Ce can effectively inhibit the discontinuous precipitation and coarsening behavior of Ag. In the Ce-free alloy, extensive precipitation of Ag-rich phases within the grains and at the grain boundaries with sizes ranging from tens to over one hundred nanometers is observed when aged at 400 °C (Fig. 5). Most of these larger precipitates show an incoherent interface with the Cu matrix. When heated to high temperatures (600 °C) in situ,                    37  discontinuous Ag precipitates in Ce-free alloys can exceed 500 nm in size (Fig. 11). The presence of coarse and discontinuous Ag precipitates is a clear indication of reduced thermal stability in the alloy. As summarized in Table 4, the discontinuous Ag precipitates have a volume fraction of 4.6% with the average diameter 73 nm in the Ce-free alloy. When subjected to the same heat treatment aging process, the addition of rare earth Ce causes the size of the Ag precipitates to decrease to one twenty-sixth of their original size, while their volume fraction increases by more than one time, as displayed in Fig. 8 and Table 4. Furthermore, these finely dispersed Ag precipitates within the grains are coherent with the matrix, and no discontinuous Ag precipitates are observed (Fig. 7). The addition of Ce promotes the formation of a greater volume fraction of the Cu4Ag(ZrCe) particles by sequestering more Ag atoms into this thermally stable intermetallic, thereby suppressing Ag coarsening at the source. Moreover, rare earth solutes retard Ag diffusion during thermal exposure, inhibiting both discontinuous precipitation and coarsening of Ag [26, 34, 39, 45]. This means that the addition of Ce can enhance the stability of the interface between Ag precipitates and the matrix. This, in turn, prevents the dissolution and coarsening of the fine Ag, strengthens the pinning effect on dislocations and grain boundaries, and ultimately suppresses rapid grain boundary migration. Zr is widely recognized for its ability to prevent the discontinuous precipitation of Ag [4, 6, 25]. By increasing the Zr content in the alloy to 0.8%, as shown in Fig. S4 and Table S1, the coarsening of Ag phases during the aging process is effectively suppressed. However, the refining effect of the rare earth Ce is more noticeable.  4.3 Role of Ce microalloying on the recrystallization behavior The presence of Ce promotes recrystallization nucleation, refines grain size, inhibits grain coarsening, and enhances the thermal stability of grains according to the in-situ heating EBSD                    38  experiment result, as shown in Fig. 12 and Fig. 13. The alloys retain the stored strain energy in their internal structures after experiencing cold deformation, and this stored strain energy, along with surface energy, serves as the driving force for recrystallization [56]. It is noteworthy that, for a given level of cold deformation applied to both alloys, the volume fraction of submicron/micron second phase particles exerts a pronounced influence on the stored strain energy[58]. The submicron/micron second phase particles collide and crush with the matrix during rolling, leading to the accumulation of significant localized strain energy[8, 58]. Adding a small amount of Ce to the alloy results in a significant increase in the volume fraction of submicron particles, which in turn improves the strain energy and enhances the driving force for subsequent recrystallization. On the other hand, the submicron Cu4AgZr/Cu4Ag(ZrCe) particles will initiate recrystallization nucleation through the particle stimulated nucleation (PSN) mechanism, which is well known and widely observed in Mg alloys [63, 64], zinc alloys [65], and aluminum alloys [66]. By incorporating rare earth elements as nanoscale precipitates in the copper alloy, such as nanoscale Cu6La [56], and Cu4Sc particles [22], it is possible to effectively prevent recrystallization nucleation and grain boundary migration. The retarding effect is attributed to the homogenization of dislocation distribution by the nanoscale particles, reducing the orientation gradient required for nucleation and consequently inhibiting nucleation [57, 58]. Conversely, incorporating rare earth elements as submicron/micron second phase particles tends to facilitate recrystallization through the PSN mechanism in the copper alloy [35, 67]. The submicron Cu4AgZr/Cu4Ag(ZrCe) phase particles typically act as preferential nucleation sites for recrystallization and play a crucial role in initiating recrystallization nucleation in this study. The number and placement of recrystallization nuclei are determined by the amount and distribution of these second phase particles. The uneven distribution                    39  of Cu4AgZr particles with a relatively smaller quantity in the Ce-free alloy results in non-uniform recrystallization PSN nuclei during heating. Adding a small amount of Ce leads to the formation of a greater quantity of submicron Cu4Ag(ZrCe) particles in the alloy. The high density of Cu4Ag(ZrCe) particles is uniformly dispersed throughout the matrix, facilitating rapid and uniform recrystallization PSN nuclei during heating. The addition of Ce not only promotes recrystallization nucleation in the alloy but also hinders the growth of recrystallized grains. The second phase particles exert a strong pinning effect (Zener pinning) on grain boundaries, with the pinning pressure being determined primarily by the size, volume fraction, distribution and stability of the particles[58]. The smaller the size of the second phase particles, the higher their volume fraction, the more uniform their distribution, and the better their thermal stability, leading to a stronger pinning force at grain boundaries, increased resistance to grain boundary migration, a slower grain growth rate, and a more significant suppression of grain coarsening [57, 58]. Hence, concerning the size and volume fraction of the second phase, nanoscale Ag precipitates demonstrate a more pronounced inhibitory impact on grain boundary migration compared to the Cu4AgZr/Cu4Ag(ZrCe) phase. The stronger pinning ability on grain boundaries and more effective suppression of grain growth is indicated by the smaller dimensions and higher volume fractions of nano-sized Ag precipitates. The addition of Ce effectively suppresses discontinuous precipitation and coarsening of Ag precipitates. The Ag precipitates exhibit a uniform nanoscale morphology with an average diameter of 2.8 nm in the Ce-containing alloy, making up 10.2% of the volume. While the size of nano Ag particles grows to 72.9 nm, and the volume fraction drops to just 4.6% in the Ce-free alloy. Additionally, it has been observed that nano-sized Ag precipitates in Ce-free alloys tends to coarsen when heated at 600 ℃ for 0.5 h (Fig. 11). However,                    40  there is no significant coarsening of Ag observed in the alloy containing Ce even after being held at 600 ℃ for 2 h. The addition of Ce significantly enhances the thermal stability of nano-sized Ag precipitates, consequently improving the high-temperature mechanical properties of the alloy. Furthermore, in-situ heating experiments have demonstrated the exceptional stability of the submicron Cu4AgZr/Cu4Ag(ZrCe) particles, as they do not exhibit any coarsening when exposed to 600 ℃ for 2 h. Consequently, the effectiveness of these precipitates in impeding grain boundaries during grain growth mainly depends on their original size, amount, and dispersion. As displayed in Fig. 5 and Table 4, adding Ce not only increases the volume fraction of the submicron particles but also effectively refines them, which is beneficial for enhancing their inhibitory effect on grain growth. This will improve the alloy's thermal stability at high temperatures. 5 Conclusions A novel copper alloy, reinforced by minor Ce, is designed to exhibit good thermal stability. The addition of trace amounts of Ce effectively enhances the high-temperature strength and plasticity, without significantly compromising the high-temperature thermal conductivity of the alloy. The influence of the rare earth Ce on the stability of the second phase and recrystallization behavior during heating is analyzed by in-situ high temperature SEM/EBSD. Conclusions drawn are as follows: (1) The addition of Ce helps to solidify free oxygen and impurity elements within the copper matrix, thus enhancing the high-temperature strength and plasticity of the alloy (2) The addition of Ce increases the population of submicron secondary phase particles and refines them at the same time. These particles act as efficient nucleation sites for recrystallization, leading to a considerable reduction in the final grain size.                    41  (3) Ce suppresses both discontinuous precipitation and coarsening of nanoscale Ag precipitates, strengthening the Zener pinning effect of the secondary phase on grain boundaries. By inhibiting grain coarsening during recrystallization, Ce markedly enhances the alloy's high-temperature mechanical properties. (4) Adding trace rare earth Ce can enhance the strength and ductility of the Cu-Ag-Zr alloy at high temperatures, with minimal impact on its thermal conductivity at 500 °C (only a slight decrease of approximately 1.5%).
